# Writing, and what strong decks do

Deck text should read as if a careful analyst wrote it close to a deadline: short, specific and plain. The lengths and
habits below are what the strong case-competition decks and pitch books behind the library actually do; every example
is invented. Each section gives the rules, then before and after pairs.

## Slide titles

- A topic label of 2 to 4 words, the length strong decks use; past 6 words it is a tagline in the wrong place. The
  claim lives in the tagline.
- Title Case, no end punctuation, no `-ing` openers, no questions.
- Name the subject in the client's terms, not a generic heading.
- Titles may repeat across a short run of slides in one section; the taglines under them never do.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| `An Overview of the Present Rival Situation` | `Rival Fare Plans` |
| `Understanding Our Riders` | `Night Riders` |

## Taglines (action titles)

- One sentence that states the finding. Strong case decks average about 10 words and pitch books about 13; nine in ten
  fall between 7 and 18. Aim for 8 to 15.
- Claim + mechanism + number where possible ("Subscription orders will lift repeat purchases from 12% to 30% of
  revenue").
- Subject first, then a concrete verb (lifts, trails, doubles, costs, depends on), then the evidence. Pitch books often
  name the causes: the result, then "driven by" two specific drivers.
- Sentence case, no full stop, no question, no colon set-up. Bold only for emphasis.
- Provable from what is on the slide. The tagline is the answer in one line (`anatomy-and-taglines`).
- Honest to the data: the verb says what the chart shows. A gap that is narrowing is still trailing, not falling
  behind; judges read the chart and pick apart an overstated tagline.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| `Analysing the drivers of declining store traffic` | `Weekday visits fell 18% after the app replaced paper punch cards` |
| `Night ridership keeps falling behind the day network` | `Night ridership still trails days, but the gap has halved since 2023` |
| `Our solution unlocks transformative value for every stakeholder` | `Same-day delivery adds $3.1M of gross profit by year 2 at a 14% margin` |
| `Strong growth prospects in a dynamic market` | `Chilled storage demand rises 9% a year, twice the pace of dry warehousing` |

## Section headers and dividers

- Panel and box headers: 1 to 4 words, Title Case, a noun phrase (`Rider Complaints`, `Evening Riders`).
- Siblings are parallel: if one header is a noun phrase, all are.
- A divider carries the section name, or the client's real question in their words; never a teaser.
- Number headers only when later slides refer back to them (`01 Fares`, `02 Routes`).

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| `Why Does This Matter?` | `Cost of Each Missed Ride` |
| `Key Learnings & Next Steps` | `What Night Riders Told Us` |

## Body text

- Lead each box with a bold phrase that carries the point, then the evidence in one or two short sentences.
- One idea per box. Specific nouns (the product, segment, region, month) over abstractions (solution, ecosystem,
  journey).
- Active voice with a named actor: who does what, by when. Recommendations use active verbs and a timeframe ("Launch a
  pilot in 3 stores in one region by Q2").
- Use the client's own vocabulary (product names, loyalty program names, terms from the prompt) over generic business
  words; it is one of the cheapest ways to pass the swap test.
- If text does not fit, cut words; do not shrink the type.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| `It is important to note that customers are increasingly seeking more convenient options` | `**Convenience wins.** 6 in 10 riders we surveyed took a slower trip to skip a transfer` |
| `The team will work to improve the sign-up experience going forward` | `**Shorter sign-up.** Cut the form from 11 fields to 4 before the spring intake` |

## Bullets

- Fragments, not sentences: no full stops, no chains of "and". Strong case decks keep bullets near 7 words; pitch books
  run to 10 or 20 but stay one clause.
- One line where possible, parallel grammar (all open with a verb, or all with a noun), at most about 4 per box.
- Open with the content word, never a filler (`Additionally`, `There is`, `In order to`).
- A number in every bullet that can carry one; no bullet restates the tagline.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| `Additionally, there is a significant opportunity to improve retention` | `Retention trails peers by 11 pts in the first 90 days` |
| `We will be focusing on expanding our partnership network` | `Sign 2 regional grocers as pickup points by Q3` |

## Captions and insight text

- Insight text is shorter than the tagline and specific to the evidence it sits on ("+21% p.a. since 2021", "Only
  segment growing", "3x the next competitor"), never a restatement of the tagline.
- A caption says what the reader sees and why it matters, in under about 12 words, sentence case, no full stop.
- Research quotes carry a role and an age band, never a real name: `Weekend shopper, 30s`.
- Timeline captions: the date, then the event in a few words (`Mar 2025: second depot opens`).

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| `This chart shows some interesting trends` | `Night trips doubled once the fare fell below $2` |

## Chart titles and axis labels

- The chart title names the measure, the unit and the base, in sentence case: `Weekly active riders (000s), 2021–2025`.
- Units once, in brackets in the title or axis label (`($M)`, `(%)`, `(x)`), not on every data label (finance-pitch
  charts carry `$` data labels, `polish`). Pitch books put the unit in the exhibit header far more often than case
  decks do.
- Years as `2024A` and `2026E` when actuals and estimates share an axis.
- No axis title where the chart title already carries the unit; never "Value" or "Category".
- The takeaway goes in an annotation on the chart, not in its title.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| `Revenue Chart` | `Sales by channel ($M), 2022A–2026E` |
| `Growth over time` | `Share of orders placed in the app (%)` |

## Footnotes and sources

- A source line on every slide with data: `Sources:` then the source types, comma-separated. Pitch books carry one on
  close to half of all their slides, title and divider slides included.
- Numbered notes `(1)`, `(2)` tie to superscripts in the body; an estimate's note states its key assumption.
- `Note:` defines a term or a basis (`Note: Fiscal year ends in June`).
- Name sources by type and date, never as a web address or "internet research".
- Quiet: 7pt, grey, bottom left (`polish` rule 4).

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| `Source: various online sources` | `Sources: Annual reports 2022–2024, team survey of 412 riders (May 2025)` |
| `*estimated` | `(1) Assumes 40% of trial users convert by month 3` |

## Numbers and units

- Specific numbers over adjectives ("+$4.2M EBITDA by the third year", not "significant upside").
- One format per deck: `$22M` or `$22mm`, `$1.4B` or `$1.4bn`; `25%`, never `25 %` or `%25`; `pts` for percentage-point
  changes.
- Round to what the decision needs: 2 to 3 significant figures on the slide, the full figure in the appendix.
- Multiples with no space (`8.5x`); ranges with an en dash and the unit once (`$40–45M`, `7.0–8.0x`). Strong decks
  write a multiple without the space dozens of times for every time they add one.
- Label estimates and periods: `2026E`, `FY25`, `Q3 2025`. Negatives as `-$1.2M` in text and `($1.2M)` in financial
  tables.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| `Revenue will grow significantly to around 15.3472 million dollars` | `Sales reach $15.3M by 2027E (+12% p.a.)` |

## Speaker notes

- Full sentences the presenter can say aloud, about 60 to 120 words per slide, roughly 45 to 60 seconds.
- Open with the slide's claim in spoken form, then the one number the room should remember, then the turn to the next
  slide.
- Carry what was cut from the slide: the method, second-tier numbers, caveats. Never read the slide text back.
- Spell out slide shorthand (`YoY`, `pts`); contractions are fine.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| `This slide shows our market analysis. As you can see, the market is growing.` | `Most of the growth sits in one place. Night trips grew twice as fast as day trips last year, and that's who our pass is for. Next, what it costs to run.` |

## IB and finance register

Pitch-book text is terser and more exact than a case deck (the full finance-pitch rules are in `polish`):

- Plain and factual: no bold mid-sentence and no callout-style phrasing.
- Noun phrases in panels and tables, not sentences (`Peer Trading Comps`, `Synergies Phasing`).
- Precise units: `8.5x LTM EBITDA`, `$1.2bn`, `+75 bps`, `22% IRR`, `2.5x MOIC`. Leverage is the debt ratio here
  (`4.0x net debt / EBITDA`), never a verb.
- Ranges, not points, for value (`$38–44 per share`, `9.0–10.5x NTM EBITDA`); a midpoint only when labelled as one.
- Periods named every time: `LTM Jun-25`, `NTM`, `FY26E`, `2024A`; label the case used (management, consensus, downside).
- Every assumption footnoted: the peer set, the price date, the case
  (`(1) As of 30-Sep-25 closing prices; peer set of 6 listed regional operators`).
- Action titles still state the finding and name its drivers, with `vs.` for comparisons. No "attractive" without the
  number that makes it so.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| `The company is attractively valued compared to its peers` | `Trades at 7.8x NTM EBITDA, a 2.1x discount to the peer median` |
| `Strong returns are expected under our base case` | `Base case: 24% IRR and 2.7x MOIC, exit at 9.0x after 5 years` |
| `The deal will be financed with a mix of debt and equity` | `Funded by $1.1bn of term loans (4.5x LTM EBITDA) and $0.8bn of new equity` |

## Names, frameworks and wordplay

- Recommendation names: 2 to 4 word branded names are memorable ("Night Ride Promise", "Fresh Shelf Pledge", "Commute
  Mixtape"), but each needs a plain-language one-liner beside it the first time.
- Frameworks are tools, not content: show SWOT, Porter's and the like only when their output drives the claim, and in
  the modified form that points to an action (weaknesses set against opportunities).
- Light wordplay is fine in moderation; never at the cost of clarity.

## Reads AI-made

These mark text as machine-written. In the strong decks read, exclamation marks and em dashes appear on fewer than 1
in 20 of roughly 1,100 slides, and question marks mostly in headers that restate the case question, in survey quotes
and on closing slides. Replace on sight.

| Avoid | Write instead |
|---|---|
| leverage (as a verb) | use, apply, build on: "use the existing van fleet" |
| unlock | the result: "adds $2M a year", "opens 3 new regions" |
| seamless | what is removed: "one tap, no second password" |
| robust | the test it passed: "held up under a 20% volume drop" |
| holistic, end-to-end | the parts covered: "pricing, staffing and stock" |
| delve, dive into | look at, test, compare |
| cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, innovative | the feature and its effect: "route planning that cuts empty miles by 15%" |
| game-changer, transformative, revolutionary | the size of the change: "halves the time to onboard" |
| empower, enable, foster, streamline (with no object or number) | the action and its target: "cut checkout from 4 minutes to 2" |
| key, crucial, vital, significant (as filler) | the number or the consequence |
| ecosystem, landscape, synergy (as decoration) | the actual parties, or the actual saving with its number |
| in today's fast-moving world, in an ever-changing market | start with the finding |
| not just X but Y; it's not about X, it's about Y | state Y |
| triplet cadence (three adjectives, three parallel clauses, slide after slide) | as many items as the evidence holds; two is fine |
| em dashes | a comma, a colon, brackets or two sentences |
| rhetorical-question headers on content slides | the answer as a claim; only a divider may carry the client's real question |
| stacked hedges (could potentially help to possibly) | one hedge with its reason: "likely, if trial conversion holds at 30%" |
| exclamation marks, emoji | nothing; let the number carry it |
| Moreover, Furthermore, Additionally as openers | drop the connector, or "and", "also", "but" |
| colon set-ups (The bottom line: ...) | the claim on its own |
| arrow characters | words or drawn shapes (`polish` rule 5) |

## Copy these

- The Problem / Recommendation / Outcome executive summary logic. It is the most reused and judge-proven slide; keep the
  logic, vary the execution.
- Section dividers phrased as the client's question.
- Spotlight build slides that fade the parent and highlight one element.
- A named persona introduced early and reused through the journey and recommendation slides.
- Numbered circles that tie the same option or recommendation together across slides.
- Insights placed on the evidence: a market slide with the target segment in the brand colour and the rest grey; a
  positioning map with a dashed note beside the client's logo; financials as big numbers with drawn arrows (case decks;
  finance-pitch decks keep figures in tables and charts).
- Every recommendation ends in numbers: cost, return, timing, KPI.
- Imagery with purpose: full-bleed title photos, circle-cropped personas, device mockups of the proposed product, real
  activation and partner screenshots, while analytical slides stay clean.
- One client-specific moment per slide: a reveal sequence, an analogy the panel will repeat, a real app mockup, one big
  number that splits the market. These are what judges remember.
- A heat-map option matrix is a good alternative to Harvey balls.

## Do not copy these

- Process taglines ("Assessing...", "Determining...", "An overview of..."). Write claims.
- The same tagline on consecutive slides.
- A dashed so-what box or bar at the bottom of nearly every slide. It becomes wallpaper and usually restates the
  tagline. A tinted key-takeaway bar is the same device and follows the same 1-in-5 limit.
- Template sameness: near-identical issue tree, SWOT and exec summary layouts with only the client swapped. They fail
  the swap test.
- Generic stock photos that could sit in any deck (handshakes, skylines with no tie to the client).
- Text below 8pt outside the footnotes; dense 5 to 7pt slides read as clutter in a room. Cut words instead.
- Overlapping elements (chart titles colliding with headers) and off-slide or cropped labels.
- Malformed numbers (`%25`), stray superscripts mid-word ("promoting1"), typos in headers.
- Placeholder artifacts left in (a lone X standing in for a number, "names" on title slides).
- A borrowed template's visual identity: take its principles, not its look.
- Long build sequences before the exec summary; keep hooks under ~30 seconds of talk time.
